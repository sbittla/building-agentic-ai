# Chapter 20: Planning and Model Routing

Give a step-by-step agent with SQL tools the question "why did revenue fall last quarter?" and watch it: it reads the schema, runs a query, runs another, reads the schema again, and twenty steps later it's still going, paying large-model prices to count rows. Nothing says what "finished" means, and nothing sends easy steps to a cheaper model. This chapter adds a plan the agent writes before it acts, which your code checks before anything runs, and a router that sends each step to the cheapest model that can do it well.

**What you'll build:** a planner that writes plans as data, a checker that rejects bad plans, an executor that runs each step with its own brief and tools and replans when a step fails, and three model routers compared by cost per successful task.

**You are here:** Planning and Model in the agent architecture reference model (section 1.7).

**Prerequisites:** Chapter 4 (agent loop), Chapter 5 (state), Chapter 19 (durable jobs), the measurement interlude.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you can:

- Choose between deciding step by step and planning first.
- Get a plan from the model as structured data, and check it in code before running it.
- Execute a plan with a small brief per step, and replan when a step fails.
- Route work between models with rules, a classifier model or a cascade.
- Compare routing strategies by cost per successful task.

**In the real world.** Coding agents write a to-do list before touching files, and deep-research systems plan before handing out subtasks (Chapter 11). Model choice is just as deliberate: in 2026, Claude Haiku 4.5 costs half as much per token as Claude Sonnet 5 (Appendix E), and open models on your own hardware cost nothing per call. Research on *routing* and *cascades* (see Learn more) shows that choosing per request can keep most of the larger model's quality at a fraction of its cost, if you measure the result.

## 20.1 Step by step, or plan first?

An agent can work in two broad ways; the second makes it the **planner** of section 1.9:

Table: Deciding step by step compared with planning first
|  | Decide step by step | Plan first, then execute |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How | After each tool result, decide the next action (the Chapter 4 loop, known in research as ReAct) | Write the steps up front, then run them |
| Good at | Short tasks, surprises, exploring | Long goals, parallel work, work you want to review or resume |
| Weak at | Staying on track over many steps; knowing when it's done | Adapting when a step reveals something unexpected |
| Cost | Every step sees the whole growing history | Each step sees only its own brief |

In practice you combine them: **plan first, execute each step with a small loop and replan when reality disagrees with the plan.** The plan also gives you something the step-by-step loop never does: a readable statement of intent that a person or a check can review before anything happens.

In {{f:plan-loop}}, follow the two loops back to the planner: a plan that fails the check is rewritten before anything runs, and a failed step sends only the remaining work back to be planned again.

Before a single tool runs, code checks everything about the plan that code can check:

@@code ch20_planning.py::check_plan,order~

It starts with **size**, because a 40-step plan for a simple question signals confusion and costs money. It accepts **known tools only**, since a step naming a tool the agent doesn't have would fail later, after money was spent on earlier steps. It checks that **dependencies are valid**: every `needs` points to a real step, and nothing goes in a circle. Python's `graphlib` finds an order that respects the dependencies, or raises `CycleError`. And it requires **a done condition for every step**.

A rejected plan goes back to the model with the reasons. It's a first look at an idea Chapter 22 builds on: **the model proposes, code decides.** The check costs microseconds and catches mistakes before they cost anything.

## 20.5 Replanning

Plans meet reality. A query fails, a table lacks the column the plan assumed, a result changes what's worth doing next. `execute` handles the first case: when a step fails, it asks for a **new plan for the remaining work**, telling the model which steps succeeded and what they returned. Finished steps are kept, and replanning has a limit.

Failure isn't the only reason to replan. **New information** can show that the plan rested on a wrong assumption ("there's no `region` column; cities are in `customers`"), and a check after each step can catch it. **A person's feedback** matters too. For expensive or risky work, having someone approve the plan (Chapter 9) is cheaper than approving every step.

Watch for the opposite failure, too: an agent that replans after every step never makes progress.

## 20.6 Plans and long-running jobs

A checked plan is exactly what Chapter 19's job runner needs: a list of steps in order. Turn each plan step into an `agent` action with its brief, and the plan gains checkpoints, retries, leases and escalation for free (exercise 20.6). That's how long agent work is usually built: **the model plans, the durable runner executes**, and a failed step leads to a replan instead of a restart.

## 20.7 Model routing

Reading a schema or counting rows is easy; explaining why revenue fell is not. Paying the largest model's price for every step wastes money, and on the local model, time. **Model routing** chooses a model per task or per step (the fourth kind of routing in this book; section 11.6 lists all four):

Table: Models to route between
| Option | Cost | Good for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A small model (Claude Haiku 4.5) | $1 in, $5 out per million tokens | Lookups, classification, formatting, simple tool calls |
| A larger model (Claude Sonnet 5) | $2 in, $10 out | Multi-step reasoning, synthesis, judgment |
| A larger model with more `effort` | More output tokens | The hardest steps: tricky analysis, careful plans |
| The local model (Appendix H) | Free, slower | Learning, private data, high volume where quality allows |

There's a second dial inside one model: **effort** (Chapter 4). A router can choose `effort: "low"` for a routine step and `"high"` for planning without changing models. For routing, Chapter 4's `run_agent` takes a `model` argument, and its stats record which model ran.

## 20.8 Three routers

@@code ch20_router.py::cost~,by_rules,by_model~,cascade

- **Rules** cost nothing and are predictable: long tasks, many tools or words like "why" and "compare" go to the large model. But they're blunt, and they miss hard tasks phrased simply.
- **A classifier model** asks the small model to rate the task. It costs one small call and catches what rules miss, but it can be wrong in both directions.
- **A cascade** tries the small model first and accepts its answer only if a **check in code** passes; otherwise it escalates. Easy tasks cost small-model prices, hard ones cost both, and with a weak check the cascade accepts wrong cheap answers.

**Fallback** routing goes the other way: it sends a request to a second model or provider when the first is overloaded or down. That's for reliability, not cost, and the fallback model needs evaluating on your tasks too.

Sending everything to the small model looks cheapest and usually isn't. On paper an always-small setup is a clear win, until you count the retries and escalations it causes. The number that matters is **cost per successful task**:

If the small model costs half as much but succeeds on 50% of tasks where the large one succeeds on 95%, it saves nothing per success (half the price for about half the successes), and failures cost extra in retries, escalations and unhappy users. :::note The support agent so far
The support agent routes by difficulty. Order-status and FAQ questions, most of its traffic, go to a small, fast model; refunds, complaints and anything the small model isn't sure about go to the larger one. Judged by cost per resolved conversation, routing cuts the bill without lowering the resolution rate.
:::

## Common mistakes

- Planning one-step questions where the loop alone is faster and cheaper.
- Plans as prose, which can't be checked or executed reliably.
- Running a plan without checking it, so an unknown tool or a cycle is found only after spending money.
- Giving every step the whole history instead of a brief with the results it needs.
- Replanning from scratch, or without a limit.
- Routing by price alone, without measuring success.
- A cascade with a weak check, which accepts the cheap model's wrong answers.

## Key takeaways

**Architect's takeaway:** A plan costs a model call and delays the first action, so use one when it saves more than it costs: on long, reviewable or resumable work.

- Decide step by step for short tasks; plan first for long ones, and replan when reality disagrees.
- Get plans as structured data and check them in code before running: size, known tools, valid dependencies, no cycles.
- Execute each step with its own brief, tools and done condition; keep finished results when replanning.
- A checked plan can become a durable job (Chapter 19).
- Route between models and effort levels with rules, a classifier or a cascade.
- Compare routers by cost per successful task, measured on real checks.
