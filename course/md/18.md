# Chapter 18: Agentic RAG and Knowledge Systems

An agent is only as useful as the facts it can find, and the way it finds them is changing. Classic retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) retrieves once and answers. An **agentic knowledge system** decides what information it needs, where to look for each piece, whether what it found is good enough, and whether to search again, then checks its own answer against the evidence. In this chapter you'll first build the retrieval machinery (chunks, embeddings, keyword and hybrid search, measurement), then put an agent in charge of it, across several sources, with evidence checks and verified citations.

**What you'll build:** a knowledge agent that plans what to look up, searches several sources, knows when it has enough evidence and checks its citations before answering.

**You are here:** Context and Memory (knowledge) in the agent architecture reference model (section 1.7).

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you can:

- Split documents into chunks that keep their source and line numbers.
- Explain what an embedding is, and choose between local, hosted and offline embedders.
- Build a vector index, a keyword (BM25) index, and combine them with rank fusion.
- Give an agent retrieval as a tool, with citations.
- Measure retrieval quality with recall@k and MRR.
- Explain how agentic retrieval differs from retrieve-then-generate.
- Let an agent plan its information needs and choose between several sources.
- Check whether the evidence is sufficient, and search again or abstain when it isn't.
- Verify an answer's citations and numbers against the evidence in code.

## 18.3 Embeddings

An **embedding** is a list of numbers (a vector) that represents a text's meaning. Texts with similar meanings get vectors that point in similar directions, so "temperature units" lands near "prefers Celsius" even though they share no words. Similarity is measured with the **cosine** (how closely two vectors point the same way). Because the vectors are normalized to length 1, the cosine is a plain dot product (`self.vectors @ q`).

Table: Embedders in the kit
| Embedder | Semantic? | Cost | Needs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `LocalEmbedder` (model2vec, potion-base-8M) | Yes, good | Free | A one-time ~30 MB download |
| `VoyageEmbedder` (voyage-4-lite) | Yes, best | Paid per token | `VOYAGE_API_KEY` |
| `HashingEmbedder` | No: similar *words* only | Free | Nothing |

`get_embedder()` picks Voyage if you have a key and the local model otherwise. If the local model can't be downloaded, it falls back to hashing. To choose explicitly, set `EMBEDDER=local`, `voyage` or `hashing` in `.env`.

:::warn Queries and documents must use the same embedder
Vectors from different models live in different spaces and can't be compared. The index file records which embedder built it; rebuild the index whenever you switch.
:::

## 18.4 Keyword search and hybrid ranking

Embeddings are weak at exact identifiers: to them, `ERR-4471` and `ERR-4417` look almost identical. **BM25** is the classic keyword ranking behind most search engines. It scores documents by how often the query words appear, giving more weight to rare words. `Index.search(mode="hybrid")` runs both searches and merges the two rankings with **reciprocal rank fusion**: each chunk scores `1/(60 + rank)` in each list, and the scores are added. ## 18.5 Retrieval as a tool

`search_knowledge` returns the top passages, each prefixed with its `(file:line)`. Wrapped as a tool, it gives you the best of both chapters: the retrieval quality of RAG, plus the agent's ability to search again with different words (or give up honestly) when results look wrong. The system prompt requires citations. Chapter 6's citation checker (exercise 6.4) needs two small changes for these answers. First, paths now start with `notes/` or `library/`. Second, a citation points to the **first line of a chunk**, so the supporting sentence may be a few lines further down. The checker in exercise 18.6 looks at the cited line and the lines after it.

## 18.6 Measuring retrieval

Before tuning prompts, check that the right passages are being found at all. `evaluate` runs 12 questions whose correct source file is known and reports two numbers per mode:

- **Hit rate@k**: the fraction of questions where the right file is in the top k results. Each of these questions has exactly one right file, so hit rate@k equals **recall@k**, the name you'll often see (and the one the code uses). When a question has several relevant documents, recall@k is the fraction of *them* found in the top k.
- **MRR** (mean reciprocal rank): the average of 1 / (position of the first correct result). Finding it first scores 1; finding it second scores 0.5.

In the kit's small corpus (collection of documents), questions share many words with their answers, so keyword search does well. The differences appear with **paraphrased** questions (exercise 18.5), which is exactly what real users ask. Keep the sample size in mind: with 12 questions, one question moves recall by 8 points, so small differences between modes are noise. Real retrieval evals use 100 or more questions, ideally taken from real user queries. And retrieval is only half the job: exercise 18.6 also checks the **answers** for correctness, valid citations and honest refusals.

## 18.7 Going further

Three techniques are worth knowing once the basics work:

- **Reranking.** Retrieve generously (say the top 30 by hybrid search), then let a **reranker** model score each candidate against the question and keep the best five. Rerankers read the question and passage together, so they're more accurate than embeddings but too slow to run over a whole collection. That's why they only see the shortlist. Voyage and others offer reranking APIs.
- **Contextual retrieval.** A chunk like "Fix: rolled back, then added 6 partitions" means little on its own. Before embedding, prepend a sentence of context generated from the whole document ("From the June 2 Kafka lag incident report:"). Anthropic's published results show this substantially reduces failed retrievals, especially combined with BM25 and reranking.
- **Access control.** This chapter's index mixes work notes and personal notes, and anyone who can ask the agent can retrieve any of it: ask the right question and the home router page comes back. In a real system, store who may see each chunk and **filter search results by the asking user's permissions before the model sees them**. Filtering in the prompt ("don't reveal personal notes") is not a control.

## 18.8 From RAG to agentic retrieval

Classic RAG is a fixed pipeline: retrieve once, then answer. It works when one search finds everything. Real questions often don't fit: "What caused the Kafka lag incident, and how many orders were cancelled in total?" needs a passage from the team notes *and* a number from the shop database, and a single vector search over the notes will never find the second.

An agentic knowledge system replaces the pipeline with a loop the agent drives:

@@image d-43480339a8ef.png

Figure: Agentic retrieval: the agent decides when it has enough
Alt: From a goal, the agent decides what is needed and where, then searches. Without enough evidence it searches again. With enough, it answers with citations; if the claims are verified it delivers, and if not it revises or abstains.

The agent decides **what** to retrieve, **where** to retrieve it from, **whether the result is trustworthy and sufficient**, and **whether another search is necessary**. The model makes those decisions; your code keeps the loop bounded (at most two rounds here) and checks the final answer. That split, the model decides and code enforces, comes back in Chapter 22.

## 18.10 Is the evidence enough?

After each round of searches, a second structured call judges the evidence *before* anything is written:

@@code ch18_agentic.py::assess

The verdict says whether the evidence answers the whole question and, if not, which searches would fill the gap. The loop runs those and assesses again, up to `max_rounds`. When the budget runs out and the evidence still falls short, the right behavior is to **answer what's supported and say plainly what isn't**: an honest "the notes don't say when ZooKeeper will be removed" beats a confident guess.

Evidence also has a trust level. A passage from the team's reviewed runbooks, a record in the orders database, a user's own statement in memory and a snippet from an unvetted web page shouldn't count the same. When sources conflict, prefer the more authoritative and the more recent (section 16.4), and when it matters, say that they conflict.

## 18.11 Verifying the answer

The model writes the answer from the evidence alone, with the origin in parentheses after every fact. Then code checks it:

@@code ch18_agentic.py::verify

Three checks catch most unsupported answers:

- **Every factual sentence has a citation.**
- **Every citation points at evidence that was actually retrieved**, not a plausible-looking file name the model invented.
- **Every number in a sentence appears in the evidence it cites.** Numbers are where invented details do the most damage, and they're easy to check exactly.

`answer` returns the problems alongside the text, so your application can revise, show a warning or abstain. Run it with `./course.sh python ch18_agentic.py`. These checks are deterministic, fast and free, which is why they run on every answer; a model-graded judge (Chapter 27) can check subtler things, such as whether a cited passage really supports the claim, on a sample.

## Architect's Takeaway

Retrieval quality determines hallucination risk: invest in ranking and citation.

## Common mistakes

- **Chunks without sources**, so answers can't be cited or checked.
- **Mixing embedders** between indexing and querying.
- **Vector-only search** for content full of codes, names and ids.
- **Never re-indexing**, so answers come from outdated documents (Chapter 6).
- **Tuning the prompt before measuring retrieval.** If the right passage isn't retrieved, no prompt can fix the answer.
- **One index for everyone**, so users can retrieve documents they shouldn't see.
- **Retrieving once and hoping**, when the question needs several pieces of information from different sources.
- **No sufficiency check**, so the agent answers from thin evidence instead of searching again or saying what it doesn't know.
- **Trusting citations without checking them**: models can cite a real-looking source that was never retrieved.

## Summary

- RAG = chunk, embed, index, retrieve, then answer with citations.
- Embeddings capture meaning; BM25 captures exact words; hybrid ranking gets both.
- Retrieval works best as a tool the agent can call repeatedly.
- Measure retrieval with recall@k and MRR before tuning anything else.
- Agentic retrieval plans information needs, chooses a source for each, judges the evidence and searches again when it's thin.
- Verify answers in code: citations must point at retrieved evidence, and numbers must appear in it.

Every agent so far finishes its task in one sitting, usually within a minute. Real work often takes longer: hours of steps, interruptions, crashes and handovers. Chapter 19 builds long-running agents that checkpoint their progress, recover from failures and keep going until the goal is met.
