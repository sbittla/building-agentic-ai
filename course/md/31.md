# Chapter 31: From Prototype to Customer Production

Lakeside Insurance's claims team wants "AI to make claims faster." They have a budget, 40 adjusters in Frankfurt, a security team whose rules were written years before you arrived, and no measurement of how long a claim file takes to read today. In a few months you'll leave, and whatever you build has to keep running without you.

Until now you owned the problem: the task, the data, the tools and the definition of done. Many agents reach production **with a customer** instead, on their data, under their rules. Chapter 30 built the platform; this chapter follows one engagement inside one customer's environment, from a vague wish to a system they can run without you. Engineers who do this are often called **forward-deployed engineers** (FDEs); `FIELD_GUIDE.md` in the course kit has their week-by-week workflow.

**What you'll build:** a field kit, `ch31_field.py`, that keeps an engagement's decisions in code: a brief checker that refuses vague goals, a model-drafted brief, a slice ranker, a design checker for the customer's rules, a pilot gate that applies signed acceptance criteria, a handoff check and a field-to-product report.

**You are here:** every layer of the agent architecture reference model (section 1.7), seen from the customer's side.

**Prerequisites:** Chapter 27 (evaluation), section 29.9 (agent economics) and Chapter 30, especially the launch checklist (section 30.7) and the agent lifecycle (section 30.13).

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you can:

- Run discovery that ends in a measurable brief with a baseline, a target and an owner.
- Break an open-ended request into slices and pick the smallest valuable one that fits the customer's constraints.
- Check an architecture against a customer's rules for data, hosting, sign-in, write access and audit.
- Run a pilot against acceptance criteria agreed in advance, and let code decide whether to promote, hold or stop.
- Hand a system over so the customer can own it, and turn repeated field fixes into features.

**In the real world.** The role began at Palantir and spread with generative AI: by 2026 OpenAI, Anthropic (as Applied AI Engineers), Scale AI, Databricks and many startups hire engineers to make agents work at a specific customer. `INTERVIEW_PREP.md` in the course kit collects the questions these teams ask.

## 31.1 What changes at a customer

When you build for a customer, the problem isn't given. A customer brings a wish and a budget, and turning that into something buildable is half the work: a problem you can't measure is a problem you can't finish. The environment isn't yours either. Their identity provider, network rules, data protection officer and messy data decide what you may build. And you leave, so a system only you can operate is a liability you've sold them.

The rest of the chapter follows the Lakeside engagement in order. Every number in it is an assumption for the example.

## 31.2 Discovery: from "we want AI" to a brief you can measure

Discovery ends when you can write a **problem brief** that a skeptical engineer, the customer's manager and their finance team would all accept:

Table: The parts of a problem brief {#t:brief-parts}
| Part | The question it answers | Lakeside's answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Users | Who does this work today? | 40 claims adjusters in Frankfurt |
| Job | What are they trying to get done? | Read a claim file before deciding the claim |
| Output | What will the agent produce or decide? | A one-page summary with citations; the adjuster decides |
| Metric | Which one number must move? | Minutes to summarize a claim file |
| Baseline and target | What is it today, measured how, and what counts as success? | 18 minutes (time study of 40 claims) to 9 |
| Customer owner | Who on their side signs off the result? | Dr. Anna Weber, head of claims operations |
| Constraints | What may the system not do, and where may data go? | Data stays in eu-central, OIDC sign-in, no writes to the claims system |
| Non-goals | What is deliberately out of scope? | Deciding claims, writing to customers, fraud detection |

Each part closes a door engagements usually fall through. Without a **baseline**, "it feels faster" is the best anyone can say afterwards. Without a **named owner**, nobody can say yes, and pilots drift for months, everyone pleased and nobody able to sign. Without **non-goals**, every meeting adds a feature. The brief checker turns these into rules:

@@code ch31_field.py::Metric,ProblemBrief,check_brief

Run `./course.sh python ch31_field.py`. The first section checks the request as it first arrived:

```
1. Discovery: is the brief buildable?
   vague brief: metric 'better efficiency' isn't measurable: give it a unit
   vague brief: no baseline: measure today's number first, or no gain shows
   vague brief: no target: agree the number that counts as success
   vague brief: no customer owner: a named person must sign off
   vague brief: no non-goals: write down what's out of scope, or scope grows
   sample brief: ready to build
```

**Measure the baseline yourself.** Watch the work if you can. An adjuster who says a big file takes "half an hour, easily" may spend twelve minutes reading and eighteen hunting for the right PDF, and those are different problems. A two-week time study is cheap next to a pilot nobody can judge.

**Let the model draft the brief.** A model is good at turning long, messy interview notes into a first draft. `draft_brief` asks for one with a forced tool call (section 3.3), tells the model to leave numbers it wasn't given as `null`, and treats the notes as data, not instructions (Chapter 25):

@@code ch31_field.py::draft_brief

`check_brief` still runs on the draft, and the model's `open_questions` become the agenda for your next call. Run `./course.sh python ch31_field.py --draft` on Lakeside's sample notes. They say a big file takes "half an hour, easily", which is an anecdote, so a good draft leaves the baseline empty and asks how it was measured.

:::warn Never invent the baseline
A model, a salesperson and a hopeful customer will all happily estimate today's number. Don't let them. A guessed baseline makes every later claim of improvement a guess, and the first person who checks will stop trusting all your numbers.
:::

## 31.3 Breaking the problem down: the smallest valuable slice

A real request is never one problem. "Make claims faster" holds a summary for adjusters, a search over past claims, letters to customers, automatic approval of small claims and checks at intake, each with its own value, cost and risk. Work through the users, their decisions, the data each decision needs and the constraints, then pick the **smallest slice that moves the brief's metric**. `rank_slices` makes that step explicit. Each slice carries the hours it saves a month (estimated from the baseline), its effort, its risk and the capabilities it needs. Slices that need something the customer has ruled out, or whose data isn't ready, are set aside with the reason. The rest are ranked by value per week of effort, discounted by risk:

@@code ch31_field.py::Slice,score,rank_slices

```
2. The smallest valuable slice:
    106.7  Summarize a claim file for the adjuster
     16.8  Search past similar claims
   ------  Draft the letter to the customer: needs email_customers, not allowed yet
   ------  Approve small claims automatically: needs core_system_write, not allowed yet
   ------  Flag missing documents at intake: its data isn't ready
```

Automatic approval saves the most hours on paper, and it's the worst first slice: it needs write access to the claims system, and a mistake costs money and trust. The summary is read-only, low-risk and moves the metric directly. Ship that, earn trust, and the next slices get easier to approve. Treat the score as a way to start the conversation. The estimates are rough; what matters is that everyone's assumptions sit on one table.

## 31.4 Designing for their environment

In your own projects you chose where the model runs and where data lives. At a customer, their security team chose years ago. Lakeside's rules came from one meeting with that team and the data protection officer:

Treat these rules like a failing test: check every design against them, in code, at every review. They add to section 30.7's launch checklist; they don't replace it. `check_design` reads a list of components and returns every rule each one breaks, with a fix:

@@code ch31_field.py::Component,check_design

The first design the team sketched broke eight rules:

```
3. Does the design fit their rules?
   [sign-in] adjuster web app signs users in with 'api-key'
      fix: use the customer's oidc identity provider
   [model hosting] agent service sends their data to a model on provider-us
      fix: use one of ['customer-vpc', 'provider-eu']
   [egress] agent service calls llm.us.provider.example, which isn't on their allow-list
      fix: remove the call, or get the host approved in writing
   [personal data in logs] agent service logs ['diagnosis']
      fix: log ids, counts and timings, not content
   [retention] agent service keeps data 90 days
      fix: keep it at most 30 days
   [residency] vector index holds customer data in us-east
      fix: run it in eu-central, or keep only ids outside it
   [write access] claims connector writes to ['claims_core']
      fix: this phase is read-only: the agent drafts, a person applies
   [audit] claims connector has no audit log
      fix: record who, what and when for every access
   fixed design: no violations
```

Every one of these came from a default in someone's quick start. The vector index ended up in us-east because that's where the free tier was, and the logs held a diagnosis because the tracing library logs prompts by default (section 28.3). Each rule maps to a chapter you've read:

Table: The customer's rules and where this book handles them {#t:customer-rules}
| Rule | What usually breaks it | Where the book handles it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Data residency | Managed services in a default region | Section 18.3 (where vectors live), section 30.6 (deployment) |
| Model hosting | A provider endpoint outside the allowed region | Section 24.6 (other providers), Appendix H (local models) |
| Egress | A tool, library or telemetry exporter calling home | Section 25.4 (exfiltration), section 30.9 (gateways) |
| Personal data in logs | Tracing that records prompts and tool results | Section 28.3 (what never goes into telemetry) |
| Retention | Caches, traces and memories kept "just in case" | Section 17.6 (forgetting), section 28.3 |
| Sign-in | API keys instead of their identity provider | Chapter 26, section 30.11 |
| Write access | A connector that can change the system of record | Chapter 9 (approvals), Chapter 22 (deterministic control) |
| Audit | Reads and writes nobody can reconstruct | Section 9.4, section 26.7 |

**When their rules seem to block the work.** Say the customer allows only models inside their own network, and the local model is weaker. Don't argue the rule; measure the trade-off. Run the evaluation suite (Chapter 27) on the model they allow and the one you'd prefer, and show both against the brief's target. Often the allowed model clears the bar for the first slice, and the question becomes "which slice needs the stronger model, and is it worth an exception?" Their security team can answer that. Messy data and legacy systems need the same discipline: an integration contract for each source (section 7.3), and tests on the ugliest data first.

## 31.5 Pilot to production

A pilot is an experiment with a question: does the agent move the brief's metric, safely, at an acceptable cost? **Before** it starts, both sides agree how to read the answer and write it down as **acceptance criteria**, signed by the customer owner:

@@code ch31_field.py::Criteria

Each line reuses a measurement you know: the success rate as a Wilson interval, p95 latency and cost per task as the scorecard defines them (section 27.6), and zero critical failures, such as a leak or a wrong payout. The one new line is the brief's metric, and it needs a word of care. The brief's target, 18 minutes down to 9, is the goal for general release. For the pilot, Dr. Weber signed a smaller bar: a 30% gain (`min_gain`), enough to show that a first slice with a small group of adjusters really moves the metric. Without that line the agent could work and still not matter. The gate applies the criteria:

@@code ch31_field.py::pilot_gate

```
4. The pilot gate (criteria signed before the pilot):
   week 2: hold: success 80%-97%, gain 39% (only 40 runs: the criteria need 50)
   week 6: promote: success 89%-97%, gain 42%
   next stage: general release
```

At week 2, 37 of 40 summaries passed: 92.5%. A demo would have declared victory. But 40 runs can't tell 92% from 80%: the interval's lower bound is 80%, under the signed 85%, and the criteria also ask for at least 50 runs before anyone decides. So the gate holds. By week 6, 150 of 160 passed, the interval's lower bound cleared 85%, and a summary took 10.5 minutes instead of 18: a 42% gain against the signed 30%, so the gate promoted. That's still short of the brief's 9 minutes, and that target belongs in the criteria for general release. Each decision commits you to something:

Table: What the pilot gate's decisions mean {#t:pilot-decisions}
| Decision | When | What you do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Promote | Every criterion met | Move to the next stage, with the gradual rollout of section 30.13 |
| Hold | Not enough evidence yet, or a fixable miss | More runs, or one change measured on the same suite (section 30.14) |
| Stop | A critical failure, or success that misses the bar even optimistically | Tell the customer owner the same day; go back to discovery or the design |

**Stages.** Most engagements move through a **proof of concept** on real data with no real users (feasibility), a **pilot** with a small group of users (value) and **general release** (scale), each with criteria agreed before it starts.

:::warn Agree the criteria before the first run
Criteria agreed after the results are in are a negotiation. Write them down, get them signed and put them in the repository next to the evaluation suite, so the gate reads the same file the customer signed.
:::

When a pilot slips, tell the customer owner early, with the cause, the new date and an option to cut scope. When they say "it's wrong 20% of the time", ask for the examples and turn each into an evaluation case (section 30.14). The field guide has both conversations.

## 31.6 Handoff

You're done when the customer can run the system without you, which is later than the day the gate promotes. `handoff_gaps` checks the pack you leave behind:

@@code ch31_field.py::HANDOFF,handoff_gaps

```
5. Before you leave:
   missing: escalation: who to call, on both sides, and how fast
   missing: training: the people who use it trained, and the people who run it
```

The technical items are section 30.7's launch checklist, already done. The handoff checks that the customer can use them: their team owns the runbook, can run the evaluation suite with the signed criteria and has tested the kill switch. Lakeside's two remaining gaps are the human ones, and they're the most common. Nobody agreed who calls whom at 3 a.m., and nobody showed the adjusters how to check a summary's citations.

## 31.7 The field-to-product loop

Every engagement teaches you something the product doesn't do yet. At Lakeside you wrote OCR for scanned PDFs by hand. At Bayview Health you wrote it again, and at Northgate Bank a third time. Three customers asking for the same fix means the product is missing a feature. `productize` counts them:

@@code ch31_field.py::FieldFix,productize

```
6. Field fixes that belong in the product:
   ocr for scanned pdfs: 3 customers, 95 hours -> build it into the product
   saml sign-in: 2 customers, 28 hours -> keep it in the field kit
   hl7 connector: 1 customer, 60 hours -> keep it in the field kit
   german date formats: 1 customer, 6 hours -> keep it in the field kit
```

The threshold is a policy; some teams promote a fix after two customers, or when the hours cross a line. What matters is that someone counts. It's section 30.14's improvement loop, applied to the product instead of one agent.

## Common mistakes

- Building before the baseline, so no gain can be shown.
- Accepting the first request as the problem. "Make claims faster" is a wish.
- Picking the most valuable slice first instead of the smallest valuable one.
- Treating the customer's rules as obstacles to negotiate instead of requirements to check in code.
- Agreeing the criteria after the results, so the pilot proves whatever the most senior person wanted.
- Declaring success from a handful of runs. 37 of 40 isn't 92%; it's somewhere between 80% and 97%.
- Leaving without a handoff, so the system fails the week after you go.

## Key takeaways

**Architect's takeaway:** At a customer, the hard rules come from their environment; write them down as data and let code check every design against them.

- A forward-deployed engineer owns one customer's outcome end to end and leaves something they can run.
- Discovery ends in a brief with a measured baseline, a target and a named owner; a model can draft it, code decides if it's ready.
- Ship the smallest valuable slice that fits the constraints first.
- Check every design against the customer's rules, in code, at every review.
- A pilot is an experiment with criteria signed in advance; a gate in code promotes, holds or stops.
- Hand over the runbook, the suite and signed criteria, a tested kill switch, escalation contacts and training.
- Count the fixes you make in the field; the ones that repeat belong in the product.

## Exercises

:::ex Concept | 31.1 | Ready to build?
For each request, say what discovery still needs before you could write a brief that passes `check_brief`: (a) "Use AI to make our call center better"; (b) "Cut the time our analysts spend on monthly reports from 3 days to 1; Priya Shah signs off"; (c) "Our lawyers want an agent that drafts contract summaries; data can't leave our network".
**Done when:** Each has its missing parts named (metric and unit, measured baseline, target, owner, constraints, non-goals) and one question you'd ask next.
:::

:::ex Simple | 31.2 | A brief that's ready
A regional hospital asks you to "use AI to reduce clinician paperwork". Pick one narrow workflow and write its `ProblemBrief`, with a metric, a measured baseline, a target, an owner, constraints and non-goals.
**Done when:** `check_brief` returns no problems for your brief, and at least four for the hospital's original request.
:::

:::ex Medium | 31.3 | What reaches the model
`check_design` asks where a model runs, not what it's sent. Add a rule: personal data may reach a model outside `customer-vpc` only if those fields are redacted first.
**Hint:** Subclass `Component` with `sends_fields` and `redacted` sets, and add your check to `check_design`'s results.
**Done when:** The fixed design's agent service is flagged when it sends names and diagnoses to `provider-eu`, is still flagged with only names redacted, and passes with the model in `customer-vpc`.
:::

:::ex Medium | 31.4 | How many more runs?
At week 2 the pilot passed 37 of 40 runs, and the gate held. Write `runs_needed(passes, runs)`: the total number of runs at the observed rate after which the interval's lower bound clears the signed 85%, or `None` if no number of runs would.
**Done when:** 37 of 40 needs about 83 runs, and 42 of 50 returns `None`; you can explain why to the customer owner in one sentence.
:::

:::ex Medium | 31.5 | Draft it, then check it
Run `draft_brief` on the sample interview notes, or on notes from a real conversation, print what `check_brief` still finds and the model's questions, then add what a follow-up call would establish and check again.
**Done when:** The draft leaves the baseline empty instead of inventing one, its open questions include how today's time was measured, and the brief passes after the follow-up answers.
:::
