# Chapter 31: From Prototype to Customer Production

Everything in this book so far assumed you own the problem: you chose the task, the data, the tools and the definition of done. Many agents that reach production are built **with a customer** instead, on their data, under their rules, for people who will judge the agent by whether their working day gets better. This chapter is the short path from a prototype to that customer's production: turn "we want AI" into a problem you can measure, pick the smallest slice worth shipping, check the design against an environment you don't control, run a pilot the customer agreed to judge in advance, and hand it over. The engineers who do this work are often called **forward-deployed engineers** (FDEs); `FIELD_GUIDE.md` in the course kit has their week-by-week workflow, meeting checklists and the conversations that keep a pilot alive.

**What you'll build:** a field kit, `ch31_field.py`, that keeps an engagement's decisions in code: a brief checker that refuses vague goals, a model-drafted brief from interview notes, a slice ranker that respects the customer's constraints, a design checker for data residency, model hosting, egress, personal data in logs, retention, sign-in, write access and audit, a pilot gate applying acceptance criteria the customer signed, a handoff check and a field-to-product report.

**You are here:** every layer of the agent architecture reference model (section 1.7), seen from the customer's side.

**Prerequisites:** Chapter 27 (evaluation), section 29.9 (agent economics) and Chapter 30, especially the launch checklist (section 30.7) and the agent lifecycle (section 30.13). This chapter adds only what a customer's environment changes; it doesn't repeat them.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you can:

- Run discovery that ends in a measurable brief: users, job, output, a metric with a measured baseline, a target, a customer owner, constraints and non-goals.
- Break an open-ended request into slices and pick the smallest valuable one that fits the customer's constraints.
- Check an architecture against a customer's rules for data residency, model hosting, egress, personal data, retention, sign-in, write access and audit.
- Run a pilot against acceptance criteria agreed in advance, and let code decide whether to promote, hold or stop.
- Hand a system over so the customer can own it, and turn repeated field fixes into product features.

**In the real world.** The role began at Palantir and spread with generative AI: by 2026 OpenAI, Anthropic (as Applied AI Engineers), Scale AI, Databricks and many AI startups hire engineers whose job is to make agents work at a specific customer. `INTERVIEW_PREP.md` in the course kit collects the questions these teams ask, with brief answers mapped to the chapters.

## 31.1 What changes at a customer

Building for a customer changes three things, and the rest of the chapter handles one at a time:

- **The problem isn't given.** A customer brings a wish ("make claims faster") and a budget. Turning that into something buildable is half the work, and it's engineering work: a problem you can't measure is a problem you can't finish.
- **The environment isn't yours.** Their identity provider, their network rules, their data protection officer and their messy data decide what you may build. You can't change them, so you design around them.
- **You leave.** Whatever you build must run without you. A system only you can operate is a liability you've sold them.

The chapter follows one engagement in the order the work happens: discovery (section 31.2), slicing (31.3), design review (31.4), the pilot (31.5), handoff (31.6) and the loop back to the product (31.7). The customer is Lakeside Insurance's claims team in Frankfurt, which wants "AI to make claims faster". Every number in it is an assumption for the example.

## 31.2 Discovery: from "we want AI" to a brief you can measure

Discovery ends when you can write a **problem brief** that a skeptical engineer, the customer's manager and their finance team would all accept. It has eight parts:

Table: The parts of a problem brief {#t:brief-parts}
| Part | The question it answers | Lakeside's answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Users | Who does this work today? | 40 claims adjusters in Frankfurt |
| Job | What are they trying to get done? | Read a claim file before deciding the claim |
| Output | What will the agent produce or decide? | A one-page summary with citations; the adjuster decides |
| Metric | Which one number must move? | Minutes to summarize a claim file |
| Baseline and target | What is it today, measured how, and what counts as success? | 18 minutes (time study of 40 claims) to 9 |
| Customer owner | Who on their side signs off the result? | Dr. Anna Weber, head of claims operations |
| Constraints | What may the system not do, and where may data go? | Data stays in eu-central, OIDC sign-in, no writes to the claims system |
| Non-goals | What is deliberately out of scope? | Deciding claims, writing to customers, fraud detection |

Each part closes a door that engagements usually fall through. Without a **baseline**, no gain can be shown: if nobody measured how long summaries took before, "it feels faster" is the best anyone can say after. Without a **named owner**, nobody can say yes, and pilots run forever. Without **non-goals**, every meeting adds a feature. The brief checker turns these into rules:

@@code ch31_field.py::Metric,ProblemBrief,check_brief

Run `./course.sh python ch31_field.py` and the first section shows what it makes of the request as it first arrived:

```
1. Discovery: is the brief buildable?
   vague brief: metric 'better efficiency' isn't measurable: give it a unit
   vague brief: no baseline: measure today's number first, or no gain shows
   vague brief: no target: agree the number that counts as success
   vague brief: no customer owner: a named person must sign off
   vague brief: no non-goals: write down what's out of scope, or scope grows
   sample brief: ready to build
```

**Measure the baseline yourself.** Talk to the people who do the work and watch it if you can: an adjuster who says a big file takes "half an hour, easily" may spend twelve minutes reading and eighteen hunting for the right PDF, and those are different problems. A two-week time study is cheap compared with a pilot nobody can judge. The field guide's discovery checklist has the rest.

**Let the model draft, and the code decide.** Interview notes are long and messy, and a model is good at turning them into a first draft of the brief. `draft_brief` asks for one with a forced tool call (section 3.3), tells the model to leave numbers it wasn't given as `null` instead of guessing, and treats the notes as data, not instructions (Chapter 25):

@@code ch31_field.py::draft_brief

The draft isn't the brief. `check_brief` still runs on it, and the model's `open_questions` become the agenda for your next call. Run `./course.sh python ch31_field.py --draft` to draft Lakeside's brief from the sample notes: the notes say a big file takes "half an hour, easily", which is an anecdote, not a baseline, so a good draft leaves the baseline empty and asks how long a file takes when someone measures it.

:::warn Never invent the baseline
A model, a salesperson and a hopeful customer will all happily estimate today's number. Don't let any of them. A guessed baseline makes every later claim of improvement a guess too, and the first person who checks it will stop trusting all your numbers.
:::

## 31.3 Breaking the problem down: the smallest valuable slice

A real request is never one problem. "Make claims faster" holds a summary for adjusters, a search over past claims, letters to customers, automatic approval of small claims and checks at intake, each with its own value, cost and risk. Work through users, the decisions they make, the data each decision needs and the constraints, then pick the **smallest slice that moves the brief's metric** and fits the constraints. `rank_slices` makes that last step explicit. Each slice carries an estimate of the hours it saves a month (say how you got it, from the baseline), the effort and a risk level, plus the capabilities it needs. Slices that need something the customer has ruled out for now, or whose data isn't ready, are set aside with the reason; the rest are ranked by value per week of effort, discounted by risk:

@@code ch31_field.py::Slice,score,rank_slices

```
2. The smallest valuable slice:
    106.7  Summarize a claim file for the adjuster
     16.8  Search past similar claims
   ------  Draft the letter to the customer: needs email_customers, not allowed yet
   ------  Approve small claims automatically: needs core_system_write, not allowed yet
   ------  Flag missing documents at intake: its data isn't ready
```

Automatic approval saves the most hours on paper, and it's the worst first slice: it needs write access to the claims system, its risk is high and a mistake costs money and trust. The summary is read-only, low-risk, and moves the brief's metric directly. Ship that, earn trust, and the next slices get easier to approve. The score is a conversation starter, not an oracle: the estimates are rough, and the point is to make everyone's assumptions visible on one table.

## 31.4 Designing for their environment

In your own projects, you chose where the model runs and where data lives. At a customer, their security team chose, years before you arrived. Lakeside's rules came from one meeting with their security team and data protection officer:

@@code ch31_field.py::Component,check_design

The first design the team sketched broke eight rules:

```
3. Does the design fit their rules?
   [sign-in] adjuster web app signs users in with 'api-key'
      fix: use the customer's oidc identity provider
   [model hosting] agent service sends their data to a model on provider-us
      fix: use one of ['customer-vpc', 'provider-eu']
   [egress] agent service calls llm.us.provider.example, which isn't on their allow-list
      fix: remove the call, or get the host approved in writing
   [personal data in logs] agent service logs ['diagnosis']
      fix: log ids, counts and timings, not content
   [retention] agent service keeps data 90 days
      fix: keep it at most 30 days
   [residency] vector index holds customer data in us-east
      fix: run it in eu-central, or keep only ids outside it
   [write access] claims connector writes to ['claims_core']
      fix: this phase is read-only: the agent drafts, a person applies
   [audit] claims connector has no audit log
      fix: record who, what and when for every access
   fixed design: no violations
```

None of these is exotic; each is a default in someone's quick start. The vector index landed in us-east because that's where the free tier was. The logs held a diagnosis because the tracing library logs prompts by default (section 28.3). Each rule maps to a chapter you've read:

Table: The customer's rules and where this book handles them {#t:customer-rules}
| Rule | What usually breaks it | Where the book handles it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Data residency | Managed services in a default region | Section 18.3 (where vectors live), section 30.6 (deployment) |
| Model hosting | A provider endpoint outside the allowed region; no local option | Section 24.6 (other providers), Appendix H (local models) |
| Egress | A tool, library or telemetry exporter calling home | Section 25.4 (exfiltration), section 30.9 (gateways) |
| Personal data in logs | Tracing that records prompts and tool results | Section 28.3 (what never goes into telemetry) |
| Retention | Caches, traces and memories kept "just in case" | Section 17.6 (forgetting), section 28.3 |
| Sign-in | API keys or local accounts instead of their identity provider | Chapter 26, section 30.11 |
| Write access | A connector that can change the system of record | Chapter 9 (approvals), Chapter 22 (deterministic control) |
| Audit | Reads and writes nobody can reconstruct | Section 9.4, section 26.7 |

**When their rules seem to block the work.** Sometimes the customer allows only models running inside their own network, and the local model is weaker. Don't argue the rule; measure the trade-off. Run the evaluation suite (Chapter 27) on the model they allow and on the one you'd prefer, and show both numbers against the brief's target. Often the allowed model clears the bar for the first slice, and the conversation becomes "which slice needs the stronger model, and is it worth an exception?", which their security team can actually answer. Messy data and legacy systems need the same discipline: an integration contract for each source (section 7.3), and tests on the customer's ugliest data first.

## 31.5 Pilot to production

A pilot isn't a demo that runs for six weeks. It's an experiment with a question ("does the agent move the brief's metric, safely, at an acceptable cost?") and an answer both sides agreed how to read **before** it started. The answer is the **acceptance criteria**, signed by the customer owner:

@@code ch31_field.py::Criteria

Each line reuses a measurement you already know: the success rate as a Wilson interval (the measurement interlude), p95 latency (section 29.5), cost per task (section 29.9) and zero critical failures (section 25.11). The one new line is the brief's metric: it must improve by the agreed amount, or the agent works but doesn't matter. The gate applies them, and code decides:

@@code ch31_field.py::pilot_gate

```
4. The pilot gate (criteria signed before the pilot):
   week 2: hold: success 80%-97%, gain 39% (only 40 runs: the criteria need 50)
   week 6: promote: success 89%-97%, gain 42%
   next stage: general release
```

At week 2, 37 of 40 summaries passed: 92.5%. That looks like a success, and a demo would have declared one. But 40 runs can't distinguish 92% from 80%, so the gate holds. By week 6, 150 of 160 passed, the interval's lower bound cleared 85%, the summary took 10.5 minutes instead of 18, and the gate promoted. Three outcomes are possible, and each means something specific:

Table: What the pilot gate's decisions mean {#t:pilot-decisions}
| Decision | When | What you do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Promote | Every criterion met | Move to the next stage, with the gradual rollout of section 30.13 |
| Hold | Not enough evidence yet, or one criterion missed by something you can fix | Keep going: more runs, or one change measured on the same suite (section 30.14) |
| Stop | A critical failure, or success that misses the bar even optimistically | Stop the pilot, tell the customer owner the same day, go back to discovery or the design |

**Stages.** Most engagements move through a **proof of concept** on the customer's real data with no real users (feasibility), a **pilot** with a small group of real users (value) and **general release** (scale), each with its own criteria agreed before it starts.

:::warn Agree the criteria before the first run
Criteria agreed after the results are in aren't criteria; they're a negotiation. Write them down, get them signed and put them in the repository next to the evaluation suite, so the gate reads the same file the customer signed.
:::

When a pilot slips, tell the customer owner early, with the cause, the new date and an option to cut scope. When they say "it's wrong 20% of the time", ask for the examples and turn each into an evaluation case (section 30.14). The field guide has both conversations, and the rules for an honest demo.

## 31.6 Handoff

The engagement isn't finished when the gate promotes. It's finished when the customer can run the system without you. `handoff_gaps` checks the pack you leave behind:

@@code ch31_field.py::HANDOFF,handoff_gaps

```
5. Before you leave:
   missing: escalation: who to call, on both sides, and how fast
   missing: training: the people who use it trained, and the people who run it
```

The technical items are section 30.7's launch checklist, already done; the handoff checks that the customer can use them: their team owns the runbook, can run the evaluation suite with the signed criteria and has tested the kill switch. The two gaps Lakeside's pack still had are the human ones, and they're the most common: nobody agreed who calls whom at 3 a.m., and the adjusters were never shown how to check a summary's citations.

## 31.7 The field-to-product loop

Every engagement teaches you something the product doesn't do yet. At Lakeside you wrote OCR for scanned PDFs by hand; at Bayview Health you wrote it again; at Northgate Bank, a third time. That's not three customer requests; it's one missing feature. `productize` counts them:

@@code ch31_field.py::FieldFix,productize

```
6. Field fixes that belong in the product:
   ocr for scanned pdfs: 3 customers, 95 hours -> build it into the product
   saml sign-in: 2 customers, 28 hours -> keep it in the field kit
   hl7 connector: 1 customer, 60 hours -> keep it in the field kit
   german date formats: 1 customer, 6 hours -> keep it in the field kit
```

The threshold is a policy: some teams promote a fix after two customers, or when the hours cross a line. What matters is that someone counts. This is the improvement loop of section 30.14 at the level of the product instead of one agent.

## Common mistakes

- **Building before the baseline.** Without today's number, no gain can be shown.
- **Accepting the first request as the problem.** "Make claims faster" is a wish; the summary for adjusters is a problem.
- **Picking the most valuable slice instead of the smallest valuable one.** The first slice earns the trust the others need.
- **Treating the customer's rules as obstacles to negotiate.** They're requirements; check designs against them in code, every review.
- **Criteria agreed after the results.** Then the pilot proves whatever the most senior person wanted.
- **Declaring success from a handful of runs.** 37 of 40 isn't 92%; it's somewhere between 80% and 97%.
- **Leaving without a handoff.** A system only you can run fails the week after you leave.

## Key takeaways

**Architect's takeaway:** At a customer, the hard rules come from their environment, not from your design; write them down as data and let code check every design against them.

- A forward-deployed engineer owns one customer's outcome end to end, in their environment, and leaves something they can run.
- Discovery ends in a brief: users, job, output, a metric with a measured baseline and a target, a named customer owner, constraints and non-goals. A model can draft it; code decides whether it's ready.
- Break the request into slices and ship the smallest valuable one that fits the constraints first.
- Check every design against the customer's rules: residency, model hosting, egress, personal data in logs, retention, sign-in, write access and audit.
- A pilot is an experiment with acceptance criteria signed in advance; a gate in code promotes, holds or stops.
- Hand over what the customer needs to own it: an owner, the runbook, the evaluation suite with the signed criteria, a tested kill switch, escalation contacts, training and known limits.
- Count the fixes you make in the field; the ones that repeat belong in the product.

That completes the chapters. The capstones come next; Capstone 7 is a whole engagement like Lakeside's, from a vague request to a signed-off pilot.

## Exercises

:::ex Concept | 31.1 | Ready to build?
For each request, say what discovery still needs before you could write a brief that passes `check_brief`: (a) "Use AI to make our call center better"; (b) "Cut the time our analysts spend on monthly reports from 3 days to 1; Priya Shah signs off"; (c) "Our lawyers want an agent that drafts contract summaries; data can't leave our network".
**Done when:** Each has its missing parts named (metric and unit, measured baseline, target, owner, constraints, non-goals) and one question you'd ask next.
:::

:::ex Simple | 31.2 | A brief that's ready
A regional hospital asks you to "use AI to reduce clinician paperwork". Pick one narrow workflow and write its `ProblemBrief`, with a metric, a measured baseline, a target, an owner, constraints and non-goals.
**Done when:** `check_brief` returns no problems for your brief, and at least four for the hospital's original request.
:::

:::ex Medium | 31.3 | What reaches the model
`check_design` asks where a model runs, not what it's sent. Add a rule: personal data may reach a model outside `customer-vpc` only if those fields are redacted first.
**Hint:** Subclass `Component` with `sends_fields` and `redacted` sets, and add your check to `check_design`'s results.
**Done when:** The fixed design's agent service is flagged when it sends names and diagnoses to `provider-eu`, is still flagged with only names redacted, and passes with the model in `customer-vpc`.
:::

:::ex Medium | 31.4 | How many more runs?
At week 2 the pilot passed 37 of 40 runs, and the gate held. Write `runs_needed(passes, runs)`: the total number of runs at the observed rate after which the interval's lower bound clears the signed 85%, or `None` if no number of runs would.
**Done when:** 37 of 40 needs about 83 runs, and 42 of 50 returns `None`; you can explain why to the customer owner in one sentence.
:::

:::ex Medium | 31.5 | Draft it, then check it
Run `draft_brief` on the sample interview notes, or on notes from a real conversation, print what `check_brief` still finds and the model's questions, then add what a follow-up call would establish and check again.
**Done when:** The draft leaves the baseline empty instead of inventing one, its open questions include how today's time was measured, and the brief passes after the follow-up answers.
:::
